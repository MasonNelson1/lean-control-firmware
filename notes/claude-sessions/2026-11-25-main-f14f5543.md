**Charles asked what the risks are, and what happens if we trip the protection board**

The danger is to the rider, not the battery. I haven't confirmed Bioenno's behaviour, but a typical protection board does this:
1. Charge current goes over its limit for a short delay (milliseconds to a second).
2. **It switches off charging only.** Power often still flows *out* of the battery, so the chair stays powered.
3. Regen now has nowhere to go. The bus voltage spikes, **both VESCs fault on over-voltage, and the motors let go.** The chair coasts in the middle of a hard stop.

**Recovery** depends on the board: it resets itself after a delay, or when current is drawn, or it stays off until the charger is plugged in. Caleb's "won't work until you plug in the charger" is real on some boards, usually after a short circuit or a fully drained battery. Ask Bioenno which kind theirs is.

**Claude**:
The key is that **there are two different currents**, and the two problems are each about a different one.

### The setup

When the chair brakes, the motors work as generators. They turn the chair's motion into electricity. The VESC sits between the motor and the battery, so there are two currents:

- **Motor current:** flows in the motor's coils. **This is what makes braking force.** More motor current means harder braking.
- **Battery current:** what the VESC pushes back into the battery. **This is what we've capped at 3 A per side**, because we thought the battery couldn't take more.

### The rule that links them

Power in equals power out (ignoring losses). Power is volts × amps on each side:

> motor voltage × motor current ≈ battery voltage × battery current

The battery voltage is basically fixed at ~53 V. **The motor's voltage depends on how fast it spins.** Fast means high voltage; nearly stopped means nearly zero.

With the battery capped at 3 A, the battery side is fixed at about 53 V × 3 A ≈ **160 W per motor**. The motor side has to fit that same 160 W. So:

> motor current ≈ 160 W ÷ motor voltage

Illustrative numbers, not measured:

| Chair speed | Motor voltage | Motor current allowed (160 W ÷ V) | Braking |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fast | ~40 V | ~4 A | Weak |
| Medium | ~16 V | ~10 A | OK |
| Slow | ~4 V | ~40 A | Strong |
| Almost stopped | ~2 V | ~80 A | Too much → fault |

### Problem 2: weak braking at speed

At speed the motor voltage is high, so 160 W only buys a few amps of motor current. Few amps means weak braking. **The 3 A battery cap is the bottleneck here.** Let the battery take more current (or dump the extra into a resistor) and you get more motor current and harder braking. **That's what raising the regen limit fixes.**

### Problem 1: the shutdown at low speed

As the chair slows, the motor voltage drops toward zero. The same 160 W now allows a huge motor current, so **the battery cap stops limiting anything**. Our firmware also tells the VESC to brake as hard as it can all the way down (the 50 RPM clamp). So the motor current shoots up past the VESC's hard safety limit of ~50 A. The VESC shuts off to protect itself and lets go of the motor for ~0.6 s, and the chair swerves.

Raising the battery limit doesn't help here, because at low speed the battery isn't the bottleneck. The battery is barely involved at that point.

**What fixes it:** limit the motor current directly. The −20 A setting we uploaded does that (still not ridden), and the proper fix is firmware that eases off braking as the chair slows instead of demanding maximum braking until it stops.

### In short

- **At speed:** the battery cap limits braking → **too weak** → fix: let more current into the battery.
- **At low speed:** nothing limits braking enough → **too strong, VESC faults** → fix: cap the motor current and ease off near a stop.
