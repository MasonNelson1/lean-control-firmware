Question 1

It says 48 V. Why do we keep saying 53 V?

"48 V" is the battery's name, its size class. It isn't what you measure across it. Our pack is 16 lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) cells in series. Each cell is officially "3.2 V", so the official pack voltage is 16 × 3.2 = 51.2 V. It gets sold as "48 V" because it drops into the same systems as a classic 48 V lead-acid pack (four 12 V car-style batteries in series).

It's the same thing with a "12 V" car battery. Nobody measures 12.0 V on one. It reads about 12.6 V resting and over 14 V while the engine charges it.

The real voltage depends on how charged the battery is. LiFePO4 has a very flat middle: from about 20% to 90% charged, each cell sits between roughly 3.25 and 3.35 V, so the pack sits around 52–53.5 V almost all the time. On 11/22 we measured 53.4–53.6 V on the court. That's the number I use in the math, because it's what the VESC actually sees.

Two lines on that chart matter for braking. The charger fills the pack to 58.2 V. The VESC's hard limit is 57 V. Right after charging, the pack is already near the VESC's limit. Braking pushes the voltage up a little more, so hard braking on a fresh pack is the one case where voltage could become a problem. Mid-charge, at 53 V, there's lots of room.

Question 2

What "motor current" actually is

Start with the one fact that everything else hangs on: a motor and a generator are the same machine. Our hub motors have coils of copper wire sitting next to strong magnets. Two things are always true at the same time:

  • Current through the coils makes force. Current in a wire next to a magnet gets pushed sideways. More current, more push. This is true no matter which way the current flows. Flip the direction and the push flips too.
  • Spinning the motor makes voltage. Moving the magnets past the coils generates a voltage across them, and the faster it spins, the higher that voltage. This is called back-EMF. On our chair it's about 7.9 V per m/s, so about 24 V at 3 m/s (estimated from the 11/22 logs).

"Motor current" just means the current flowing in those coils. Because current in the coils is what makes force, motor current is the force: double the motor current, double the push on the wheel. Whether that push speeds the chair up or slows it down depends on which way the current flows.

Driving

This is the picture you had: the battery supplies current, the VESC sends it into the motor, and the push spins the wheel forward. That's correct, for driving.

Braking

When braking, the battery doesn't send anything. The spinning wheel turns the motor into a generator. The VESC lets current flow out of the coils, and because current in the coils always makes force, that outgoing current makes a push backwards against the spin. That's the braking force. The energy it takes out of the chair's motion goes back into the battery.

So the fix to the sentence you were building: motor current is the current in the motor's coils, and it is the braking force. When braking, it flows out of the motor, and the VESC decides how much to let out. Let more out and you brake harder. That current then has to go somewhere, and the only place is the battery. That's battery current.

Why would those two currents ever be different? Because the VESC doesn't just pass current through like a wire. It works like a transformer for DC: it trades voltage for current. Which brings us to the one rule.

The rule

Same power on both sides, different volts and amps

Energy can't appear or vanish inside the VESC (apart from some heat, which I'll ignore). So the power coming out of the motor equals the power going into the battery. Power is volts times amps:

motor volts × motor amps = battery volts × battery amps

Now put in what's fixed. The battery side is pinned: about 53.5 V, and we've capped battery current at 3 A per motor because we thought the battery couldn't take more. So the battery side can never take more than 53.5 × 3 ≈ 160 W per motor.

The motor side has to fit inside that same 160 W. But the motor's voltage isn't fixed: it depends on speed. So:

motor amps allowed = 160 W ÷ motor volts = 160 W ÷ (7.9 V per m/s × chair speed)

7.9 V per m/s is the motor's generator constant: every 1 m/s of chair speed makes 7.9 V across the motor (estimated from the 11/22 logs). The same constant gives the force: each amp of motor current pushes the wheel with about 7.9 N.

Fast means high motor voltage, so 160 W buys only a few amps: weak braking. Slow means low motor voltage, so the same 160 W buys a lot of amps: strong braking, then far too strong. Drag the slider and watch.

Problem 2 · the fast end

Braking is too weak at speed

At 3 m/s the motor makes about 24 V. Divide the 160 W the battery can take by 24 V and you get only ~6.7 A of motor current. Motor current is braking force, so the chair brakes gently right when you most want it to brake hard.

The bottleneck is the battery cap. Let the battery take 7.5 A instead of 3 A (400 W) and the motor current at 3 m/s goes up to ~17 A, about 2.5× the braking force. That's the "raise the regen limit" fix, or the brake resistor, which is just another place to send that power.

Problem 1 · the slow end

The VESC faults near the end of a stop

Below about 1 m/s the motor makes only a few volts, so 160 W would allow 20, 40, 80 A. The battery cap stops limiting anything. Our firmware (the 50 RPM clamp) tells the VESC to brake as hard as it possibly can all the way down. So when the cap lets go, motor current surges, overshoots the VESC's hard limit of about 50 A, and the VESC shuts off to protect itself. It releases that motor for ~0.6 s and the chair swerves.

Raising the battery cap doesn't help here, because the battery isn't the bottleneck at low speed. The fix is to cap the motor current itself (the −20 A setting already uploaded, not ridden yet), then firmware that eases off the brake as the chair slows.

The numbers behind this page

  • Battery 53.5 V: measured 53.4–53.6 V on 11/22. Battery cap: −3 A per VESC today (6 A total).
  • Motor voltage 7.9 V per m/s: estimated from an 11/22 log line (1690 ERPM at 13.7% duty on 53 V) and the earlier note that 900 ERPM ≈ 0.49 m/s.
  • Chair + rider 120 kg: an estimate. Both motors brake equally until a fault.
  • The simulation ignores losses. Real motors turn some braking energy into heat in their own coils, which adds a bit of braking, so real stops are a little shorter than shown.
  • The fault surge is drawn as a simplified spike to ~55 A where the cap lets go (about 0.6 m/s with the old −32.7 A limit). The 11/22 logs show the faults happened during hard stops but don't pin the exact speed.
  • Fix 1 not faulting is what we expect. It hasn't been ridden yet.
