1. Executive Summary:
The purpose of this project is to solve a clear limitation in current sports wheelchairs. Right now, the athlete must use their hands to move the chair, which directly interferes with passing, shooting, and ball handling during play. Our project aims to modify an existing sports wheelchair so that it can be controlled through leaning, allowing the user to keep both hands available while still moving quickly and safely. This is intended to improve athletic performance in wheelchair sports like basketball and pickleball. 
Our current design uses two tightly connected systems. The first is an electrical system that senses the user’s lean, processes that input, and controls power delivery. The second is a mechanical system that mounts the drive components to the existing wheelchair and transfers power to the wheels. Together, these systems are intended to provide hands-free movement while prioritizing the safety, stability, and controllability of the chair. The current prototype concept uses a pressure-sensor seat array, onboard control electronics, dual motor controllers, two BLDC drive motors, a belt-reduced drivetrain, a dog coupling quick-disconnect wheel system, and an onboard 48 V battery.
Our planned design adapts an existing sports wheelchair by adding drive, sensing, and control systems in a way that preserves the chair’s agility, stability, and familiarity to the user. The current concept also preserves limited manual repositioning and transportability, prioritizes durable mounting for repeated impacts, and avoids choices that would make the wheelchair unnecessarily bulky, unsafe, or difficult to maintain.
The main risks to the project are purchasing, integration, and overall system complexity. Major components must work together reliably for the wheelchair to perform as intended, and this intricacy creates risk in both control and mechanical implementation. Custom fabricated parts also introduce some risk because they must fit correctly, withstand repeated impacts, and remain practical to assemble and maintain. In addition, the cost of motors, batteries, sensors, and supporting hardware increases the overall cost of the system and may affect long-term feasibility depending on future production goals.
One expected limitation of the lean-controlled sports wheelchair is that athletic body motion and motion-command input are closely linked. For example, reaching laterally to intercept a basketball pass may also create a lean input that commands wheelchair motion. The current design philosophy accepts that the user will need practice to adapt to this new style of play, while future control tuning may reduce unintended motion through calibration, deadbands, and command shaping.
The design analysis indicates that the selected architecture can meet the major performance, runtime, packaging, and safety requirements under the stated assumptions, but prototype testing is still required to validate real-world control behavior, structural durability, and battery runtime.
Overall, this project presents a meaningful opportunity to improve wheelchair athletics by freeing the user’s hands without giving up safety or control. The current design direction shows a practical path toward a working system that could improve competitive play, expand what is possible for wheelchair athletes, and provide clear value to the user and other stakeholders.
2. Problem Statement:
The project sponsor is funding the development of a prototype lean-controlled sports wheelchair for a wheelchair athlete ("the rider") with lower-limb paralysis who competes in court sports such as basketball and pickleball. In these sports, the athlete must normally use their hands to propel and maneuver the wheelchair, which directly interferes with passing, ball handling, and other athletic actions during play. This creates a significant limitation because movement and gameplay both depend on the athlete’s upper body. The project therefore seeks to develop a sports wheelchair that uses controlled upper-body lean to command motion while remaining stable, durable, and practical for competitive use. The central challenge is to provide hands-free maneuvering without sacrificing the safety, responsiveness, and on-court usability required in wheelchair athletics.

6. Background Information and Research
6.1 Sports Wheelchair Design:
Sports wheelchairs differ significantly from standard daily-use wheelchairs because they are optimized for agility, stability, and rapid maneuvering during athletic play. Their geometry, materials, and seating systems are selected to improve court performance rather than long-term comfort or general indoor accessibility.
One of the most noticeable differences is wheel geometry. Standard wheelchairs commonly use little to no camber, while sports wheelchairs often use rear wheel camber angles in the range of approximately 12 to 20 degrees [10]. This wider wheel stance improves lateral stability and supports quicker turning, which is valuable in sports such as wheelchair basketball. A tradeoff of this wider configuration is that it can increase the overall width of the chair, which reduces compatibility with narrow doorways or other indoor spaces [10].
Sports wheelchairs also commonly use large rear wheels, typically around 25 to 26 inches in diameter [11]. A larger wheel covers more ground per rotation, which can benefit sustained movement, while a smaller wheel generally has lower rotational inertia and can feel more responsive during acceleration. In addition, sports wheelchair tires tend to have a lower profile than those used on standard wheelchairs, which contributes to quick handling on smooth court surfaces. The wheel structure is also designed for contact-heavy use. Spokes are often reinforced, and protective wheel covers may be added to reduce damage during collisions or entanglement with other chairs [11]. Hand rims are shaped for quick and forceful contact during play and are typically more durable than comfort-oriented daily-use rims, although this often comes at the expense of long-term comfort [12].
Frame and seating materials are also selected with athletic performance in mind. Sports wheelchairs are commonly constructed from aluminum, titanium, or, less frequently, carbon fiber [13]. Aluminum is widely used because it offers a favorable combination of strength, low weight, stiffness, cost, and manufacturability. Titanium is often used in higher-end chairs because it offers excellent strength-to-weight ratio, fatigue resistance, corrosion resistance, and vibration damping, but it is more expensive and more difficult to manufacture. Carbon fiber can provide extremely low weight and high stiffness, but it is less common due to cost and concerns about brittleness or impact durability in contact-intensive applications [13].
The seating system of a sports wheelchair also differs from that of a traditional wheelchair. Sports wheelchairs commonly use a bucket-style seat that helps limit unwanted lateral body motion and improves containment during aggressive movement [13]. The seat front is often raised relative to the rear, typically by about 5 to 15 degrees, which helps position the athlete securely and can improve balance and stability during turning [13]. Padding is generally kept minimal because excessive cushioning can allow the athlete to shift too much during impacts or rapid maneuvers. High-tension straps and other containment features are often used to help keep the athlete securely positioned in the chair during competitive play [13].
Taken together, the sports wheelchair is a purpose-built athletic platform designed around rapid acceleration, turning, repeated contact, and secure rider positioning. This is important to keep in mind because any added propulsion and control system must preserve as much of that athletic functionality as possible without compromising it.
6.2 Current Electric Wheelchair Control Methods:
Powered wheelchairs are most commonly controlled through proportional joystick systems. One source reports that approximately 80% of powered wheelchairs use joystick-based control [14]. In this type of interface, the user commands speed and direction through joystick displacement, typically using a hand-mounted control located near an armrest. These systems are widely used because they provide intuitive proportional control and can be tuned through parameters such as sensitivity, acceleration ramps, and dead zones [15]. When such parameters are not properly configured, however, the wheelchair may become difficult to control or behave in a way that is less safe for the user [15].
Although joystick control is effective for many users, it depends on sustained upper-body function, fine motor control, and continuous hand use. For many everyday mobility applications this is acceptable, but it is not ideal in situations where the user’s hands must remain available for other tasks. In athletic settings, this limitation becomes especially important because the same upper-body motion needed to propel or steer a conventional wheelchair may interfere directly with ball handling, passing, or defensive movement.
Alternative control methods exist for users who cannot rely on standard hand-operated interfaces. Sip-and-puff systems use changes in air pressure through a tube-like interface to generate control commands [16]. These systems can be highly valuable for users with severe limb limitations who retain respiratory control. Other alternatives include head-array systems, which use switches positioned around the headrest, and chin-operated joystick systems, which allow the user to command the chair through head or chin motion rather than hand motion. These approaches expand accessibility to a broader range of users, but they are generally developed for reliable daily mobility rather than high-speed athletic maneuvering.
Different control methods involve tradeoffs among proportionality, precision, responsiveness, fatigue, training burden, and resistance to unintended activation. Joystick systems provide strong proportional control, but they require hand use. Sip-and-puff systems can be reliable and accessible, but they may impose training demands and can cause fatigue under extended or intensive use. Head-array and chin-operated systems can provide effective mobility access, but they depend on stable head motion and may be more sensitive to unintended movements. Many alternative systems also rely more heavily on switch-like inputs than on continuous proportional inputs, which can limit fine control during rapid maneuvering.
Safety is a central requirement across all wheelchair control interfaces. Standards and established design practice emphasize reliability, fault tolerance, prevention of unintended motion, and preservation of controllability under abnormal conditions. Control systems must minimize false activation while still remaining responsive enough for safe maneuvering. Redundant shutdown mechanisms and fail-safe defaults are commonly integrated to reduce user risk in the event of sensor, controller, or power-system faults.
Existing control methods show that wheelchair input design is a question of how motion is commanded, how reliably user intent is interpreted, how much fatigue the interface imposes, and how safely the system behaves when disturbances or faults occur. These tradeoffs are especially important for a lean-controlled sports wheelchair because the intended application demands athletic freedom and safe, intuitive motion control.
6.3 Target User Populations:
Wheelchair basketball classifies athletes according to functional ability, particularly trunk control and movement capability during play. Players are assigned classifications from 1.0 to 4.5 in increments of 0.5, and each team must remain within a total classification-point limit while on the court [17]. In general, lower classifications correspond to less trunk control and less functional reach, while higher classifications correspond to greater trunk mobility and balance during athletic movement [18].
At the lower end of the classification range, players have little to no active trunk control and are more limited in their ability to lean, rotate, or recover balance during play. As classification increases, players generally gain greater ability to lean forward, rotate the torso, and, at the higher classifications, lean laterally with more control [18]. The half-point classifications are used for athletes whose functional ability falls between the major whole-number classes.
This classification framework is important for the present project because a lean-controlled wheelchair depends on the user being able to produce intentional torso motion in a controlled and repeatable way. A user must be capable of leaning enough to generate a meaningful input signal, while also maintaining sufficient balance and posture control for safe athletic operation. For that reason, the concept is most naturally suited to athletes with relatively high trunk function. Based on the functional demands of lean-based control, the most appropriate target population is likely toward the upper end of the wheelchair basketball classification range, approximately classes 3.5 through 4.5. These users are more likely to have the forward, rotational, and lateral trunk motion needed to operate a lean-controlled system effectively.
This does not mean that athletes in lower classifications could never benefit from alternative hands-free control strategies. Rather, it indicates that the specific control concept considered in this project is not equally appropriate for all wheelchair athletes. Users with more limited trunk function would likely require a different control architecture, such as one based on head, chin, or respiratory input, in order to achieve hands-free mobility safely and effectively.
In addition to functional classification, the athletic environment itself affects the target-user discussion. Court sports involve rapid starts, abrupt stops, close-quarters maneuvering, and repeated contact with other athletes and wheelchairs. These conditions place demands not only on the propulsion system, but also on rider stability, seating security, and control robustness. The intended user population must therefore be considered not only in terms of available trunk motion, but also in terms of whether that motion can be used repeatedly and safely under realistic sport conditions.
Taken together, these considerations show that the target user population for a lean-controlled sports wheelchair is narrower than the full population of wheelchair athletes. The concept is best aligned with users who have substantial trunk control and who can intentionally shift posture in multiple directions during dynamic play. Defining this target population clearly helps bound the design problem and supports later decisions related to sensing, control logic, stability, and safety.

7.3 Control Requirements for Competitive Use 
7.3.1 Analysis Objective 
The purpose of this subsection is to identify the control-performance demands imposed by competitive court sports on a lean-controlled wheelchair. Unlike a normal powered wheelchair, the proposed system must translate torso motion into propulsion and steering commands while the user is engaged in athletic tasks such as passing, receiving, reaching, and avoiding contact at the same time. The control problem involves generating motion from body lean in a way that remains intuitive, responsive, stable, and resistant to unintended activation during competitive play. 
7.3.2 Modeling Assumptions and Context 
Several assumptions are made in order to analyze the control problem at a solution-neutral level. The intended operating environment is a flat indoor court surface used for sports such as wheelchair basketball. The intended user population has sufficient trunk control to produce meaningful forward and lateral torso lean. During normal athletic use, the wheelchair will experience both deliberate user-generated posture shifts and unplanned disturbances such as collisions, abrupt stops, and rapid balance corrections. It is also assumed that upper-body motion during sport is not always intended as a command to the wheelchair, since the athlete may lean significantly while catching, passing, contesting the ball, or recovering posture.
These assumptions create a more demanding control problem than that of a normal daily-use powered wheelchair. A standard control system only needs to respond accurately to explicit input commands. In this case, however, the control system must infer driving intent from body motion that may serve both athletic and navigational purposes. As a result, the system must balance responsiveness with selectivity so that the chair remains intuitive to operate without becoming overly sensitive to normal sport-related movement.
7.3.3 Representative Competitive Control Scenarios 
Competitive wheelchair sport suggests at least two primary control scenarios and one important non-command scenario.
The first scenario is close-quarters maneuvering. This occurs in and around active play, where the athlete must reposition rapidly for passes, defend nearby opponents, or create small openings for movement. The wheelchair must support fine directional control, rapid turning response, and predictable behavior at low to moderate speeds. Small changes in user posture should produce smooth and controllable changes in motion rather than abrupt or exaggerated commands.
The second scenario is court transition. This occurs when possession changes or when the athlete must move quickly across the court. Strong forward acceleration and stable high-speed control are more important than tight maneuverability. The control system must allow the athlete to command aggressive forward motion without introducing instability, oscillation, or excessive steering sensitivity.
The third scenario is non-command athletic motion. During gameplay, the athlete may lean forward to receive a pass, laterally to intercept the ball, or abruptly in response to player contact. These motions may resemble intentional drive inputs even when no wheelchair motion is desired. This creates a fundamental design choice in the control philosophy. One approach is to make the wheelchair adapt to the athlete’s existing playstyle by attempting to distinguish athletic posture changes from true drive commands. The other approach is to allow these lean motions to produce wheelchair response and instead expect the athlete to adapt to the new movement possibilities and constraints introduced by the chair. Either we design the chair to adapt to the player, or the player must adapt to the chair. In practice, the final design may fall somewhere between these two philosophies, but the control system must be developed with this tradeoff in mind because it strongly affects responsiveness, intuitiveness, and the overall style of play the wheelchair will support. 
Taken together, these scenarios show that the control requirements of the wheelchair are not defined by responsiveness alone. The system must also preserve controllability across a wide range of operating conditions and minimize false activation during normal athletic movement.
7.3.4 Control Implications for a Viable Solution 
The control problem defined above leads to several important solution-neutral conclusions. First, the wheelchair must provide both fine low-speed maneuverability and stable high-speed response. The control behavior required for close-quarters play is not necessarily the same as that required for full-court transitions. A viable solution will therefore need a control strategy that remains usable across these different operating regimes.
Second, the controller must address the relationship between athletic body motion and drive intent. Because torso motion during sport does not uniquely indicate a desire for wheelchair propulsion, the final design must adopt a deliberate control philosophy. One approach is to reduce unintended activation by distinguishing athletic posture changes from true drive commands through thresholds, deadbands, filtering, or command shaping. Another approach is to allow a stronger coupling between body lean and wheelchair response, accepting that some athletic motions will produce movement and that the user will adapt to the new playstyle this creates. A viable solution may lie between these extremes, but the tradeoff must be considered explicitly because it strongly influences the design approach. 
Third, the control system must remain stable and predictable when disturbances occur. Competitive wheelchair sport includes contact, rapid posture correction, and abrupt directional changes. A viable control system must therefore tolerate transient disturbances without producing unsafe or confusing motion. This requirement is especially important because the user’s body serves both as the control input source and as part of the physical system being disturbed.
Finally, the control system must preserve a safe fallback operating condition. Because the wheelchair is intended for competitive use, loss of control authority or unintended motion presents a significant safety concern. Any viable design must therefore provide a means of quickly reducing or removing commanded motion if the sensed input becomes unreliable or if continued powered operation becomes undesirable.
Overall, this analysis shows that the control problem for a lean-controlled sports wheelchair is fundamentally different from that of a typical powered wheelchair. The controller must do more than respond quickly to body motion. It must balance responsiveness, maneuverability, high-speed stability, disturbance tolerance, and resistance to false activation. These conclusions establish the problem-level control demands that later design choices must satisfy.
8. Standards & Regulations:
This section identifies the external standards, test methods, and sport-specific rules most relevant to the design of the lean-controlled sports wheelchair. Because the project combines a powered mobility system with a competitive athletic wheelchair, the design is constrained by both powered-wheelchair safety standards and wheelchair-basketball equipment rules. 8.1 Determination of static stability
ISO 7176-1:2014 [21], Wheelchairs — Part 1: Determination of static stability.
Description: 
This standard specifies the requirements for static stability under several conditions such as a flat hard platform with an adjustable slope that is large enough to accommodate the wheelchair to be tested. Along with this it has requirements on the need for slide restraints to prevent an unlocked wheel or anti-tip device from rolling that still prevents a locked wheel from moving or sliding.
Impacts:
This standard establishes the baseline for testing the safety of the wheelchair in static positions and ensures that the chair remains reliable and safe while in a static state.
Adjustable platform:
The platform must be adjustable and lie between two imaginary parallel planes 5mm apart with no more than a 0.5 degree angle of variation in slope of or cross slope throughout the test. Must also have a roll restraint to prevent an unlocked wheel from rolling.
Slide Restraint:
The slide restraint must prevent a locked wheel or anti tip device from sliding that does not affect the wheelchair’s freedom to tip about the contact point of the restrained wheel or post.
Tipping Limiter:
The tipping limiter to limit the extent of tipping of the wheelchair relative to the test platform that does not affect the stability of the wheelchair, restrict the wheelchair’s freedom to deform, or restrict the wheelchair’s freedom to tip sufficiently to detect a zero force under the uphill wheels of the wheelchair.
Angular Measurement:
The angular measurement device to measure the angle of the slope of the test platform along with a test dummy that must be secured by the torso, thigh, and lower leg portions in the wheelchair.
8.2 Determination of dynamic stability of electrically powered wheelchairs
ISO 7176-2:2017 [22], Wheelchairs — Part 2: Determination of dynamic stability of electrically powered wheelchairs.
Description:
This standard specifies testing methods and performance requirements related to the dynamic stability of electrically powered wheelchairs. It includes a range of defined procedures that evaluate whether a wheelchair can remain upright while in motion, accounting for factors such as acceleration, turning, momentum, and operator input.
Impacts:
This standard establishes the baseline expectations for the dynamic stability of the wheelchair design. Compliance ensures that the wheelchair will remain stable during normal operation, even when subjected to changes in speed, direction, and operator movement.
Dynamic Stability Testing:
The standard defines a variety of stability tests intended to simulate real-world operating conditions. These tests evaluate whether the wheelchair maintains balance and avoids tipping while accelerating, decelerating, or maneuvering. Meeting these requirements ensures that the wheelchair will stay upright during typical usage scenarios.
Surface Inclination Considerations:
Some procedures within the standard involve testing the wheelchair on inclined surfaces to evaluate stability on slopes. Since the intended application for this wheelchair is indoor basketball on a flat gymnasium floor, the inclined-surface portions of the testing procedures may be considered less critical for the final design validation.
Control System Stability Constraints:
Because the wheelchair will be lean-controlled, the stability requirements in this standard provide important constraints for the control system design. The system must ensure that operator leaning inputs do not cause unsafe tipping conditions and that the chair remains within stable operating limits regardless of how the user shifts their weight.
Design Safety Implications:
Adhering to this standard ensures that the final wheelchair design maintains safe operating characteristics during dynamic movement. These requirements guide the mechanical design, center-of-mass placement, and control system behavior to ensure the wheelchair remains stable and safe for athletic use.
8.3 Determination of effectiveness of brakes
ISO 7176-3:2012 [23], Wheelchairs — Part 3: Determination of effectiveness of brakes.
Description:
This standard specifies testing procedures and performance requirements for the braking capabilities of wheelchairs. It defines methods for evaluating both static and dynamic braking performance to ensure that the wheelchair can remain stationary when required and safely stop while in motion.
Impacts:
This standard establishes the baseline safety expectations for the braking system of the wheelchair. Compliance ensures that the chair can reliably stop and maintain control during operation, which is critical for safe use during athletic activities.
Static Braking Performance:
One of the primary tests defined in the standard evaluates the effectiveness of the brakes when the wheelchair is stationary. This test measures the maximum slope on which the wheelchair can remain at rest without sliding when the brakes are engaged. While this test verifies the holding capability of the braking system, it is less critical for this design because the wheelchair is intended for use on a flat indoor gym floor.
Dynamic Braking Performance:
The second major test evaluates braking performance while the wheelchair is moving at its maximum velocity. This procedure measures the distance required for the wheelchair to come to a complete stop once the brakes are applied. Meeting this requirement ensures that the operator can maintain control of the wheelchair during high-speed movement and sudden stopping scenarios.
Brake Actuation Force Requirements:
The standard includes specifications for the force required to engage the braking system, typically presented in tabulated form. These values provide guidance for designing the brake mechanism so that it can be reliably activated while still remaining accessible and practical for the operator.
Design Safety Implications:
Adhering to this standard ensures that the wheelchair braking system provides consistent stopping performance and prevents uncontrolled movement. The requirements guide the selection and design of the braking components to maintain safe control of the wheelchair at all times during operation.
8.4 Energy consumption of electric wheelchairs and scooters for determination of theoretical distance range
ISO 7176-4:2008 [24], Wheelchairs — Part 4: Energy consumption of electric wheelchairs and scooters for determination of theoretical distance range.
Description:
This standard defines testing procedures used to evaluate the energy consumption and maneuverability performance of electrically powered wheelchairs during operation. The tests measure how efficiently a wheelchair uses energy during continuous driving as well as while performing maneuvering tasks representative of real-world use.
Impacts:
This standard provides a framework for assessing how efficiently the wheelchair converts stored electrical energy into motion. Compliance helps ensure that the wheelchair can operate for an adequate duration while maintaining the level of maneuverability required for athletic environments such as wheelchair basketball.
Continuous Driving Energy Test:
One of the primary tests measures the energy consumption of the wheelchair during continuous motion. In this procedure, the operator drives the wheelchair in a circular path for a specified period, and the energy consumed during this activity is recorded. The results provide a metric for evaluating the overall energy efficiency of the propulsion system during sustained movement.
Maneuvering Energy Test:
A second test evaluates energy consumption during maneuvering tasks. In this procedure, a course is established using cones, and the wheelchair must navigate between them. The spacing between the cones is used in the calculation of performance metrics. Wheelchairs that are capable of tighter turning and more precise maneuvering will perform better in this test, as they can complete the course more efficiently.
Design Performance Considerations:
Because the wheelchair is intended for basketball gameplay, the ability to move quickly across the court while maneuvering around other players is essential. These tests provide useful performance metrics for evaluating how well the design supports rapid directional changes and efficient motion.
Design Efficiency Implications:
Adhering to this standard helps ensure that the wheelchair design balances energy efficiency and maneuverability. The results guide decisions related to motor selection, drivetrain configuration, and control system design, reinforcing that efficient operation involves more than simply traveling long distances in a straight line.
8.5 Lead-acid batteries and chargers for powered wheelchairs
ISO 7176-25:2022 [25], Wheelchairs — Part 25: Lead-acid batteries and chargers for powered wheelchairs.
Description:This standard specifies safety and performance requirements for lead-acid batteries and their associated charging systems (rated up to 250 V AC input and 36 V nominal output) designed specifically for electrically powered wheelchairs and scooters. It ensures that the battery set and charger are compatible, reliable, and safe during normal use as well as foreseeable misuse.
Impacts:
Regardless of the final battery solution used, this standard establishes the baseline safety and performance criteria for any lead-acid power solution being considered for the final solution of the wheelchair. 
Risk Assessment:
The design process must include a formal risk analysis in accordance with ISO 14971. This analysis must account for storage and operating temperature, as well as any foreseeable misuse. 
Environmental Ingress Requirements:
Any charging solution integrated into the design must meet specific Ingress Protection (IP) ratings. For indoor basketball use, a minimum of IPX1 is required, but if the chair is used in non-dry environments, IPX4 protection must be ensured. 
Mechanical Fatigue Constraints: 
Any on-board charging or battery housing components must be designed to survive the multi-drum and kerb-drop fatigue tests defined in ISO 7176-8. This is a significant constraint for a basketball wheelchair, as the mounting system must prevent connections from working loose during frequent on-court collisions.
Interface and Connector Selection:
 If a lead-acid system is utilized, the charger must conform to IEC 60335-2-29 and utilize standardized connectors, such as the XLR type defined in IEC 61076-2-103. The design must also include reverse polarity protection to prevent electrical hazards during user-led maintenance.
Charger Compatibility and Intelligence: 
The standard requires that the charging system be capable of identifying a faulty battery set and preventing unsafe charging. Our future control system will need to support these performance-related safety indicators to inform the athlete of the system status.

Description:
This international standard establishes specific safety requirements and test methods for lithium-ion batteries, battery management systems, and charging systems used in electrically powered wheelchairs. It addresses unique risks associated with Li-ion chemistry, such as thermal runaway and fire propagation. It standardizes how these systems should perform during both stationary charging and active wheelchair operation (including regenerative charging during deceleration).
Impacts : 
Because the wheelchair basketball design is motorized and uses a lean-control system, compliance with ISO 7176-31:2023 is critical for both user safety and system reliability under high-performance conditions, particularly if a lithium-ion battery were used in the final solution.
Safety and Fire mitigation: 
The standard requires that a thermal runaway event within a single cell must not result in a fire of the entire battery system or propagate outside the housing. For a contact-heavy sport like wheelchair basketball, the battery enclosure must be designed to withstand impacts (as per cross-referenced ISO 7176-8) while maintaining this fire containment.
Battery Management System (BMS):
The design must include a BMS that maintains safety by preventing damage and managing cell imbalances. The BMS must communicate with the wheelchair controller to report cell voltages, temperatures, and state of charge. 
Connector Standardization:
If the design includes an external charger, the standard specifies requirements for charger connectors, such as the XLR type. This ensures communication between the charger and control system and prevents electrical hazards during the charging cycle.
8.7 NWBA Official Rules
Wheelchairs — Part XX: NWBA Official Rules (Rule 1, Section 2: Player Equipment) [27]
Description:  
This Document establishes the legal specifications for wheelchairs used in competitive wheelchair basketball. It ensures a level playing field by regulating wheelchair dimensions and safety features, such as seat height, wheel size, and bumper placement, to prevent injury or damage to the court.  
Impacts: 
The NWBA standards could act as the primary geometric constraints for our lean-controlled wheelchair. Following these rules is essential for the chair to be as close to “game-legal” as possible and safe for interacting with other players in basketball. 
Chassis and bumper Design: 
The requirement that the front horizontal bar be no more than 11cm off the floor dictates the mounting height of our front end assembly. This will ensure that during a collision, the wheelchair will not “ride up” over other players' wheels/bumper and cause problems.  
Drive System and wheel selection: 
The restriction on the rear wheel diameter (max 69cm including tires) directly impacts our motor torque calculations. We must select motors and gearings that provide the necessary acceleration for the sport while fitting within this specific wheel diameter.
Center of gravity and stability:
The regulations regarding seat height (max 58cm to 63cm, depending on player classification) and rear caster height (no more than 2cm from the surface) create a narrow window for our lean-control sensitivity. Since our design relies on "lean" to drive, we must ensure that the "tilt" required to trigger the sensors does not cause the seat rail to exceed the 21-inch height limit or cause the rear anti-tip casters to touch the ground prematurely, which would stall the chair's agility.
 Integrated Safety:  
The NWBA rules regarding "no sharp edges" and "protection of the floor" mean that our motorized drivetrain and battery housing must be fully encased in non-marking, rounded protective shrouding to prevent leaking battery fluids or metal edges from damaging the court or injuring participants.

10. Ethical and Social Analysis:
The primary ethical lens for this project is virtue ethics. In this context, virtue ethics emphasizes responsible design choices that reflect prudence, care, and a strong commitment to the safety of both the user and the people around them. This has been a central concern throughout the design process. Because the wheelchair is intended for competitive athletic use, the design must balance speed and responsiveness with stability and controllability. For that reason, the team has focused on limiting unsafe behavior through features such as a maximum speed limit, an emergency shutoff, movement only when a user is detected in the chair, and power cutoff in the event of a tip or flip. These measures reflect the importance of designing the system in a way that protects the user even in worst-case scenarios.  This emphasis on anticipating hazards and implementing protective measures is consistent with Deuteronomy 22:8, which describes the responsibility to prevent foreseeable harm through thoughtful design and precaution. 
The two secondary ethical lenses are rights-based ethics and utility-based ethics. Rights-based ethics emphasizes the user’s right to reasonably safe equipment and the family’s right to understand the risks and behavior of the system being used. This reinforces the importance of adhering to relevant safety standards, verifying calculations and specifications, and maintaining transparency with the user and family about how the wheelchair operates and what its limitations are. Utility-based ethics focuses on achieving beneficial outcomes for the user. This perspective also aligns with 1 Corinthians 10:24, which encourages individuals to seek the good of others rather than acting only for personal benefit.  In this project, that includes allowing the athlete to move without using their hands for propulsion so they can focus more fully on gameplay, ball handling, and rapid response to changing conditions on the court.
Together, these ethical lenses show that the design must support safe operation for the user, reduce unnecessary risk to other players and the surrounding environment, and preserve fair participation in sport. Fairness is especially important because a system that behaves in unexpected or poorly understood ways could create confusion or increase the risk of injury during competition. Proverbs 11:1 also reinforces the importance of fairness and integrity, supporting the need for predictable and equitable system behavior during athletic participation. Overall, the design process has aimed to prioritize user safety, protect others who interact with the wheelchair, and provide a clear and accessible safety mechanism for emergency situations.
11. Design Description:
11.1 System Overview
Our modifications to the wheelchair are organized into two major subsystems: the electrical system and the mechanical system. These tightly integrated systems work together to support intuitive, fully lean-controlled, and safe operation of a sports wheelchair. Figure 6 shows a render of the full CAD model, including the motors and battery.Figure 6: Overall design of the Lean controlled Wheelchair
The electrical system is responsible for central processing, lean-input sensing, user configuration, manual input detection, and electrical energy storage. The mechanical system mounts the drive components to the existing wheelchair, transfers torque from the motors to the wheels, and preserves the structural and transport features needed for athletic use.
To detect lean inputs, the design uses a pressure sensor array on the seat. The pressure sensors provide values that correspond to the operator’s pressure distribution, which the controller uses to estimate lean direction and magnitude. These inputs are directed to the Teensy 4.1 microcontroller for processing. Other electrical inputs include the power switch, user adjustment dials for maximum speed and acceleration, and a calibration button. An emergency stop, implemented through a modified circuit breaker, forms the main disconnect between the battery and the electrical system. The block diagram also accounts for disturbance inputs such as collisions with other players on the court. To address these disturbances, the control algorithm may use filtering, damping, or closed-loop compensation to reduce unsafe or unintended motion.
Once the controller has processed the inputs, it determines the motor commands required to produce the desired wheelchair motion. The motor controllers draw power from the battery and regulate power delivery to the motors, forming the main connection between the electrical and mechanical systems. The selected motors are paired with a 8:1 planetary gearbox and an 80:44 timing-pulley reduction, producing an overall gear ratio of 14.55:1. Torque is transferred through the belt drive to the dog coupling mechanism and then to the wheels, producing the wheelchair motion shown as the output of the block diagram.

11.3 Electrical Overview
The electrical system senses user lean, processes operator inputs, manages safety controls, and commands power delivery to the drive motors. The major electrical components include the seat-mounted pressure sensor array, analog-to-digital converters, Teensy 4.1 microcontroller, user-interface controls, VESC motor controllers, BLDC motors, emergency stop, and onboard battery.
11.3.1 Lean-Sensing System
The lean-sensing system uses 4 pressure sensors placed in the cardinal directions on a metal plate below the seat cushion. These 4 sensors collect pressure-distribution data with enough resolution for lean detection without adding unnecessary complexity or latency to the system.
The lean-sensing system uses a 4x4 pressure sensor array placed on either the top of or below the seat cushion, depending on the results of prototyping. These 16 sensors collect pressure-distribution data with enough resolution for lean detection without adding unnecessary complexity or latency to the system.

The pressure sensor array estimates the user’s pressure centroid across the seat. As the operator leans, the force distribution shifts across the array, allowing the controller to infer lean direction and relative lean magnitude.
The farther the pressure centroid shifts from the neutral position, the farther the operator is leaning. The selected lean sensors are capable of detecting small pressure changes, making the system precise, inexpensive, and replaceable. The sensors can also be repositioned on the cushion to better match different operator sizes. For a smaller operator, the sensors can be placed closer together; for a larger operator, they can be spread farther apart while still remaining under the seated user.
11.3.2 Control Hardware and User Interface
Figure 9 shows the major electrical connections between the lean sensors, analog-to-digital converters, Teensy 4.1 microcontroller, user-interface controls, VESC motor controllers, and battery. This schematic provides the electrical-system layout used to supply power and information throughout the electrical system.
The lean-control sensors connect to 16-bit analog-to-digital converters, which convert the sensor voltages into digital values that can be interpreted by the microcontroller. As discussed in the sensor-system analysis section, the selected sensors can withstand the load from the operator and produce voltage outputs within the interpretable range of the ADCs. The resulting signal resolution and measured latency of approximately 18 ms support the response requirements for lean-based control. 
The Teensy 4.1 microcontroller serves as the central controller for the electrical system and will be housed in an electrical enclosure to protect the wiring and connections. The Teensy has enough GPIO capacity for the planned lean sensors and user-interface inputs, while still leaving room for later feature expansion. It operates at a 600 MHz clock rate and can handle interrupts effectively, which is useful for a system that must respond quickly to user input and safety-related events.
The Teensy is supplied with 5 V power and provides a 3.3 V source for compatible sensors, buttons, and ADCs. The 16-bit ADCs transmit sensor data to the Teensy through an I2C bus. After processing the input data, the Teensy communicates motor commands to the VESC motor controllers through a CAN bus using a CAN transceiver.

The design includes a personalization panel to allow the operator to adjust wheelchair behavior within the safe performance limits of the system. In addition to the personalization controls, the electrical system includes an emergency stop, power button, and calibration button. The power button provides a non-emergency way to turn the wheelchair on and off, allowing the user to safely enter, exit, or reset the chair. The calibration button resets the neutral lean position by zeroing the sensor readings while the operator is seated in a neutral posture. This reduces drift and allows the operator to recalibrate the chair in the event that drift occurs. 
In addition to the physical emergency stop, the software will stop sending drive commands if it detects a sensor fault, motor fault, motor-controller fault, or other unsafe input condition. This supports the fail-safe behavior required for safe operation during athletic use.
11.3.5 Battery Integration
The design uses two VESC 6 motor controllers, one for each drive motor. These controllers regulate power delivery from the battery to the brushless DC motors based on commands from the Teensy microcontroller. This makes the motor controllers the main electrical interface between the low-power control system and the high-power propulsion system.
Figure 9: Schematic of the Electrical Components
The VESC controllers also connect directly to the hall sensor wires from the BLDC motors. This allows the controllers to monitor motor position and improve motor-control performance without requiring the Teensy to handle low-level motor commutation. Reducing this processing burden allows the Teensy to focus on interpreting user input, applying control logic, and managing safety behavior.
Each VESC controller also includes onboard sensing features, including an internal IMU. These signals may be useful for detecting abnormal wheelchair behavior such as excessive tilt, impact response, or loss of traction, although the final use of these features will require further validation during testing. The combination of motor-control capability, feedback options, and existing documentation makes the VESC 6 a practical controller choice for the lean-controlled wheelchair.
11.3.6 Battery and Motor Integration
In compliance with Spec U, all propulsion batteries, motor controllers, and required electronics are mounted on board the wheelchair during operation. The selected battery is a Bioenno Power lithium iron phosphate battery with a nominal voltage of 48 V and a capacity of 30 Ah. This battery includes a built-in battery management system and is evaluated in Section 12.3 for its ability to support the runtime requirement in Spec O.
The battery is rated for 30 A continuous current and 60 A peak current for short-duration loading. These current limits are important because the wheelchair is expected to experience brief high-power demands during acceleration, turning, and braking events. The electrical design must therefore ensure that motor-controller settings and operating limits remain compatible with the battery’s allowable current output.
The propulsion system uses two 450 W brushless DC motors. Brushless DC motors were selected because they provide good efficiency, reduced internal friction, and improved longevity compared with brushed DC motors. Since the system already uses a DC battery supply, BLDC motors also simplify the overall power distribution. Operating at 48 V reduces the current required for a given power level compared with lower-voltage systems, which helps reduce electrical losses.
11.3.7 Control Box
The control box is a user interface to determine how the wheelchair works based on user input. The control box consists of five buttons and two dials. The buttons let the chair turn on, calibrate to the user, set the mode to lean control, set the mode to controller mode, and set the mode to user controlled mode. These let the user have full control over how the chair is controlled and when it works. The dials control the speed and acceleration of the chair to determine how fast the chair is for user comfort and getting used to the chair or using it in a slower mode when not playing the game. This data is fed to the TEENSY controller and determines these parameters for the control algorithm.
11.3.8 Electronic Box
The design includes a box for containing the base electronics that the user does not interact with. These systems include the motor controllers, main system PCB, sensor connections, and TEENSY. The motor controllers determine how much current is transformed into AC for the motors to spin at a set speed of choosing. The main system PCB includes all the electrical connections so that wiring is contained and not going to be knocked loose while in use. The sensor connections are then connected to the PCB and are fed as information to the TEENSY to determine what needs to be done on the motor controllers to properly translate the information being received into proper movement. 
11.3.9 Braking 
The design will also utilize the motors for braking. This braking is achieved by generating torque with the motors to oppose the wheel rotation to reduce speed in a controlled manner. This reduces the design complexity and overhead cost that would have occurred with a mechanical braking system. This approach supports Spec P by allowing safe and powerful deceleration.
11.4 Mechanical System
11.4.1 Mechanical System Purpose
The mechanical system provides the structural interface between the existing sports wheelchair and the added propulsion system. Its primary functions are to mount the motors and drivetrain components, transfer torque from the motors to the wheels, preserve quick wheel removal for transport, and maintain the stability and durability required for athletic use.
The mechanical design directly supports the specifications related to impact durability, stability, user safety, transportability, and manual handling. The motor and wheel mounting system is designed to be rigid enough to withstand expected sport-contact loading while still allowing the wheels to be removed without tools. The original caster layout is retained to preserve the wheelchair’s resistance to tipping, and the final design will include edge finishing and shielding where needed to eliminate exposed sharp edges, burrs, and accessible pinch points.
11.4.2 Wheelchair Frame Interface
Figure 10: CAD Render of the Motor Mount
The mechanical assembly attaches to the existing wheelchair through four custom clamps, shown in red,  and two braces in teal, with two clamps mounted on each side of the frame and the braces in the back. The clamps attach to the main frame tubes and provide flat mounting surfaces for the added drivetrain structure. 11.4.3 Motor and Drivetrain Layout
Several motor placement options were considered, including direct drive at the wheel, mounting the motors under the front of the seat, and mounting the motors behind the main tube in the rear of the wheelchair. Direct drive was rejected because it would require significant structural modification to the existing wheelchair and would require a much more compact motor and gearbox package than the current design. Front mounting was also considered early in CAD development, but it created packaging conflicts with the battery, reducing available space under the seat. The rear-mounted motor layout was selected because it provides a space efficient method for packaging the motors, drivetrain, battery, and frame supports while preserving the core geometry of the existing wheelchair.
Three gearbox approaches were considered: spur, planetary, and worm gears. Worm gearing was rejected because it is impossible to backdrive, which precludes the ability to have manual movement. A custom spur gearbox would be possible, but would add design, manufacturing, lubrication, and safety complexity. A commercial off-the-shelf planetary gearbox was selected because it is compact, self-contained, professionally manufactured, and already matched to the selected motor.
The selected propulsion system uses two 450 W brushless DC motors. On top of the motors is a 8:1 planetary gearbox which feeds into a 80:44 timing-pulley reduction, producing an overall gear ratio of 14.55:1. Based on the motor and drivetrain performance analysis in Sections 12.2.2 through 12.2.5, this configuration is intended to provide the torque and speed needed to satisfy the forward acceleration requirement in Spec F, maximum speed requirement in Spec G, turning performance requirement in Spec H, and motor-braking requirement in Spec P.
Torque is transferred from each motor to the wheel through a 15 mm wide, 5 mm pitch HTD timing belt. The motor-side pulley has 44 teeth, and the wheel-side pulley has 80 teeth. A belt drive was selected because it provides high torque capacity, low noise, low maintenance, and minimal slip when properly tensioned. Compared with a chain drive, the timing belt is better suited for this compact layout because it avoids chain lubrication, reduces noise, and provides smoother operation over the short center distance used in the design.
Due to safety concerns of getting body parts stuck in the motor and pulley layout, covers were designed to keep those components out of view and reach of the user without removing the covers. These covers consist of two 3D printed parts acetone welded together to create a full cover. This design was mirrored to be on both sides of the chair to protect the user and others from getting hurt while attempting to use or set up this wheelchair.
11.4.4 Motor Mount and Support Structure
The motor and wheel-side drivetrain components are mounted to the same motor mount plate to maintain alignment between the gearbox output shaft, belt drive, dog coupling, and quick disconnect pin. Keeping these components on a common plate helps ensure that the pulley axes remain parallel and that belt alignment is maintained during operation. The bearing has a pivot to loosen or tighten the motor mount to align all the components and keep the support structure secure and adaptable depending on need.
The motor mount plate attaches to the main boxtube structure through custom angle brackets and supporting hardware. The clamps are designed to provide a strong connection to the existing wheelchair frame, but the design also includes a custom 3d print that supports the motors to not bend the rear support plate due to impact. This improves the stiffness and robustness of the drivetrain mounting system during impacts, acceleration, braking, and turning while still maintaining the effectiveness of the motors. 
The outer dog clutches were press fitted into the wheel hub. This effectively allowed the dog clutches to be fused with the wheel hub in a way that did not require any bolts, welding, or adhesive connecting force. This was the method chosen as the dog clutches needed to be permanently inserted into the wheel hub where other connection methods were not secure or safe.

The support structure is intentionally designed with removable bolted connections. This allows the prototype to be adjusted during fabrication and testing while still providing the stiffness required for drivetrain alignment and sport-contact loading. This was preferred to welding as welding was deemed impractical due to the permanence of the welds. 
11.4.5 Battery Mount
The battery will be housed in a bent sheet-metal enclosure mounted directly under the seat. This location keeps the battery away from the user’s legs, places the battery near the electrical system, and maintains the overall center of mass of the wheelchair. The battery being directly under the seat is beneficial as it keeps the battery out of all direct contact from the user and other outside interference that may interact with the battery.
The battery will be attached in two parts. The two parts consist of one connected to the bottom of the seat, and the second mounted to the frame of the chair to create an enclosure to house the battery while in use. This provides secure mounting while distributing loads into the frame. The enclosure must restrain the battery during vibration, impacts, and rapid wheelchair motion, while still allowing reasonable access for charging, inspection, or replacement.

11.4.8 Dog Coupling and Quick-Disconnect Wheel System
The dog coupling system allows each wheel to be removed from the wheelchair without tools while still transferring motor torque to the wheel during powered operation. This system was modeled after the existing quick-disconnect wheel mechanism so that the modified wheelchair preserves the original transport functionality as much as possible.
Figure 13: Torque Transfer Path Exploded View
A key design feature is that drive torque is not transmitted through the removable wheel shaft. Instead, torque is transmitted through the keyed pulley and dog coupling plates. This avoids the manufacturing difficulty of using a tapered or hex shaft as both a removable wheel axle and a torque-transfer element. It also preserves the intuitive, toolless quick-disconnect behavior of the original wheelchair.
When the wheel is removed, the inner dog coupling plate remains supported by bearings in the motor mount assembly. The drive pulley connects to the keyway on the inner dog clutch and is retained with setscrews and adjacent spacers to keep the belt in place while preventing tearing. The bearings support the dog clutch radially and keep the drivetrain components aligned when the wheel is not installed.Figure 14: Section View of Dog Coupling Mechanism
When the wheel is installed, the removable quick disconnect pin passes through the coupling assembly and locks in place using the original quick-disconnect mechanism. The outer dog coupling plate is press fit into the wheel hub fusing them together, while the inner and outer dog coupling plates engage to transfer torque. The shaft and retaining ring constrict longitudinal loads, while the dog coupling transfers motor torque into the wheel hub. The radial load is shared between the dog clutches and quick disconnect pin. This allows the wheel to remain removable while still supporting powered operation.
The dog coupling plates are custom steel components made from an old hydraulic cylinder. The design uses 3 in. diameter steel round stock for the coupling body, and a smaller welded shaft section where needed. The welded connection is as good as it can get by human hands, but still allows the wheel to wobble because it is not fully concentric. This approach reduces wasted material compared with machining the entire part from a single oversized piece of stock. The coupling teeth will be CNC machined, and the shaft and coupling body will be welded using a custom fixture to maintain alignment.
The coupling geometry is inspired by the function of commercially available jaw-style couplings, such as Lovejoy couplings, but the design omits the elastomer insert because this application requires minimal backlash. The wheelchair operates at low rotational speed, transmits relatively high torque, and changes direction frequently. For that reason, minimizing backlash is important for responsive control and reducing impact loading during direction changes.
A three-tooth coupling pattern was selected as a balance between strength, manufacturability, and engagement reliability. The inner coupling plate includes a 1 in outer shaft section and a 0.5 in bore for the removable shaft. The outer coupling plate is similar, but its outer diameter is sized to interface with the existing wheel hub. The final press-fit diameter may require adjustment after precise measurement of the hub bore.

12.1.2 Control Loop and Software Architecture
12.1.2.1 Program Overview
The Teensy 4.1 microcontroller runs the control software as a fixed-rate interrupt-driven loop executing at 1 kHz. This means the control logic is triggered by a hardware timer interrupt once every 1 ms rather than running as a free-running loop. The fixed-rate approach ensures consistent timing between sensor reads, centroid computation, and motor commands regardless of how long any individual step takes, which is important for predictable and stable control behavior. Background tasks such as monitoring incoming CAN status frames from the two VESCs are handled in the main program body outside the timed interrupt and do not affect control loop timing.
The software executes in three distinct phases: an initialization phase that runs once at power-on, a recurring 1 ms control loop that handles all sensing, computation, and motor commanding during normal operation, and a fault response routine that can be triggered at any point during the control loop if a safety condition is detected. Figure 18 illustrates the overall program flow. This fixed-loop structure supports predictable sensor processing and command timing, which is necessary for the wheelchair to feel consistent and controllable during athletic use.
Figure 18 — Software flow diagram showing initialization phase, 1 ms control loop with sequential execution phases, and fault branch
12.1.2.2 Initialization Phase
When the Teensy powers on, the software completes hardware initialization before the control loop begins. This includes configuring the I2C bus for communication with the four ADS1115 ADCs, initializing the CAN peripheral and MCP2562 transceiver for communication with the two VESCs, and reading the initial positions of the speed and acceleration adjustment knobs. Default baseline values for all 16 sensors are initialized to zero, allowing the system to power on without requiring immediate calibration. However, normal use is intended to begin after the rider performs calibration from a neutral seated posture. The illuminated power switch is activated at the end of initialization to signal to the rider that the system is ready.
12.1.2.3 Main Control Loop — 1 ms Execution 
Each 1 ms loop cycle proceeds through five sequential phases.
The first phase is sensor acquisition. All 16 FSR readings are collected from the four ADS1115 ADCs over I2C. The ADCs operate in continuous conversion mode so that a fresh reading is always available when the Teensy requests it, avoiding mid-loop conversion wait times.
The second phase is a calibration check. The firmware checks whether the calibration button is currently pressed. If it is, the raw reading of each sensor at that moment is stored as that sensor's baseline value, and the loop advances to the next cycle without computing a centroid or sending a motor command. This prevents a partial or transitional centroid from being transmitted during the snapshot instant. Normal operation resumes on the following cycle.
The third phase is baseline subtraction and fault evaluation. The stored baseline value for each sensor is subtracted from its current raw reading to produce a baseline-corrected value. The corrected readings are summed across all 16 sensors to produce a total cushion load. If this total falls below the ejection detection threshold, the fault response routine is entered immediately, and the remaining phases of the current cycle are skipped. VESC-reported motor temperatures, received via CAN status frames in the background, are also evaluated at this stage. If either motor exceeds the thermal derating threshold, the commanded current limit for that motor is scaled back accordingly.
The fourth phase is centroid computation and command mapping. The baseline-corrected sensor values are used to compute a pressure centroid position as described in Section 12.1.2.7. The centroid's forward-rearward component drives the speed command for both motors, and the centroid's lateral component drives the differential split between left and right motor current. A deadband is applied around the neutral centroid position so that small unintentional shifts do not produce motion. The speed and acceleration knobs are read each loop cycle and applied as scaling factors on the centroid output, giving the rider real-time adjustment of the chair's overall responsiveness without changing the underlying centroid logic.
The fifth phase is motor command transmission. Left and right current commands are encoded into CAN frames and transmitted to the two VESC 6 motor controllers. Both frames are sent within the same 1 ms cycle.
12.1.2.4 Calibration Routine
The calibration button allows the rider to set a personalized baseline that accounts for their individual seated weight distribution on the cushion. Because the FSR sensors respond to absolute force rather than relative changes, riders of different weights or seating positions produce different raw readings in a neutral, upright position. Pressing the calibration button while seated normally captures that neutral distribution as the reference zero for all subsequent centroid calculations. This allows the centroid calculation to reflect intentional lean relative to the rider’s neutral posture, rather than simply responding to absolute pressure differences across the cushion. Calibration can be performed at any time without powering the chair off, and the chair will not move during or immediately after the calibration press because the command transmission phase is skipped for that cycle.
12.1.2.5 Fault Response and Safe State
When a software-detectable fault condition is detected, such as rider ejection from the cushion load monitor, the firmware immediately transmits explicit zero-current commands to both VESCs over CAN before ceasing further command transmission. For a hard emergency-stop activation through the circuit breaker, the circuit breaker directly removes propulsion power from the system as described in Section 12.1.4.1. Sending an explicit zero command rather than simply stopping transmission ensures the VESCs receive an active instruction to reduce output rather than relying on a communication timeout to infer that the controller has stopped. After the zero command is sent, the current decay function described in Section 12.1.4.1 governs the ramp-down of motor output. The system does not automatically resume normal operation following a fault. Power must be cycled to re-enter the initialization phase and return to normal control loop execution.

12.1.4.2 User Ejection / Seat-Occupancy Detection
The firmware monitors total cushion pressure by summing all 16 baseline-corrected sensor readings. If the total pressure signal falls below 200 ADC counts, the system interprets this as a possible loss of operator contact with the seat and applies the current-decay safe-state response. The threshold was selected to be above the electrical noise floor (< 50 counts measured with cushion unloaded) while well below the minimum loading produced by a seated rider. This threshold will need to be validated during physical testing to ensure normal athletic leaning does not falsely trigger the safe-state response.

At 80°C the multiplier is 1.0 — no derating. At 90°C the multiplier is 0.5 — current is halved. At 100°C the multiplier is 0 — the output is cut to zero. Between 80°C and 100°C the transition is continuous and proportional, avoiding an abrupt torque step that could destabilise the chair.

This asymmetric behaviour is intentional — a symmetric cutback would reduce total drive force unnecessarily when only one controller is thermally stressed.
The VESC 6/75 hardware also has its own independent firmware-level thermal protection that will cut output and set a fault flag if the MOSFET temperature exceeds the VESC Tool configured limit. The firmware derating layer described here acts as a gentler upstream intervention that reduces current demand before the VESC's own hard limit is reached, avoiding sudden fault-state stops during gameplay. This derating strategy protects the motor controllers from overheating while allowing reduced operation before a full thermal shutdown is required.

13. Feedback Items
During the presentation of this design and its subsequent review we have received various pieces of valuable feedback which will be strongly considered and implemented into the design in the future. The people who gave feedback are Dr. Nardos Ashefani, a robotics and controls specialist at Bastian Solutions in Boise, Dr. O’Hern and Brian Higgins, our instructors, and Dr. Aaron Smith, a mechanical engineer with his PE stamp. 
13.1 Mechanical System Feedback
13.1.1 Dynamic component AlignmentFigure 30: Side view of dynamic components
One large concern involves aligning the dynamic components of the drivetrain, specifically the bearings. To ensure that the shaft runs smooth and true, it is imperative that the drivetrain components be precisely aligned with little room for tolerance. If the components are not aligned, the inner deg coupling plate will bind at best, and at worst, be impossible to assemble.  The reason this is such a large concern is because of stacking tolerances in our design. You can see in figure 30 that both bearings have the blue boxtube as a shared reference, but the mount plates in grey and pink and angle brackets in teal are all somewhat complicated parts that have multiple levels of features that must all be precise in order to achieve a good fit. Another complicating factor is that the angle brackets are currently slated to be welded onto the boxtube and bolted to the motor mount plates.  This choice was made because of difficulties with getting threaded fasteners to fit the space constraints, but it comes at the cost of imprecision. The primary mitigation strategy for these issues is to be really careful when machining. Dr. Smith suggested consulting with the professional machinist Phil Boysen who works in the EIS at Boise State University for more advice on solving this issue. Another possibility, if budget allows, would be sending the parts to be professionally machined by a company such as Send Cut Send. 
13.1.2 Steel vs. Aluminum Shafts and Fatigue
Steel shafts were recommended as the material of choice for the shafts in the drivetrain, as opposed to aluminum because aluminum does not have an infinite life fatigue limit. Our shafts are undergoing intense fully reversed cyclic stress. Driving across the length of a court once requires 15 revolutions of the wheel. If you roll across the court once every 10 seconds, which works out to around 5mph, and do that continuously for 2 hours, the wheel’s shaft will undergo around 10k cycles. Especially over multiple games, fatigue is a serious issue and this makes aluminum shafts unacceptable. Thus we will use steel for all our dynamic rotating components. 
13.1.3 Motor Cantilever SupportFigure 31: Sketch of possible cantilever support option
Currently the motors are only supported by the motor mount plate. This can be seen in figure 31, While that plate itself is well supported, the motors have a lot of mass and will most likely bend the plate due to the significant impacts the chair will continually undergo. To mitigate this, it is recommended that we design some sort of support for the back of the motors. This will certainly be implemented once we have the motors on hand and can iterate a 3D printed support to accomplish this. The support does not have to be extremely strong, it simply has to mitigate the bending loads caused by the motors. 
13.1.4 Miscellaneous
Because this is a human operated device a safe testing plan must be developed. It is very likely that things will go wrong initially and it is best if they go wrong without a human in the chair. 
Hannah said that disk brakes are probably not a viable solution because of the added complexity, cost, and electrical draw. We should conduct and document some brief analysis to confirm this. 
The motors we are currently designing around are sourced from China which raises purchasing concerns. Due to the tight budget we need to be aware of potential tariffs when buying these. 
The weight of the system and inertia of the drivetrain could make manual movement of the wheelchair significantly difficult. While this is inevitable, analysis could be done to attempt to quantify this impact. Depending on the result of that analysis it may be propitious to pursue greater weight reduction. 
13.2 Electrical System Feedback
13.2.1 Sensor Placement and Interference
Placing the sensors in the correct location will make or break the design. Having a direct and intuitive connection to the chair is the entire purpose of this project. Our sponsor Dan clarified that having a hard plate directly under the cushion, will not negatively affect the playing experience but also brought up the concern that placing the sensors near the top of the cushion exposes them to the sweat and abrasion of the user. Beyond the physical placement of the sensors, we were also reminded of the Garbage In, Garbage out principle.  Nardos mentioned that these sensors will likely be prone to noise and Mr. Higgins expounded on the effects of electrical interference on the sensor readings. Because of the high currents passing to the motors from the battery and through the motor controllers, there may be electromagnetic noise picked up by the sensors. Thus it's critical that we ensure we get good readings. A potential solution to this is using differential sensing, an alternative protocol that is similar to what CAN bus uses. Ensuring the wiring is shielded can also mitigate these effects.
Another issue with the sensor placement was the sheer number of wires there are. We are planning on starting with four sensors placed in the cardinal directions, each of which has 2 wires and # soldered connections. Mr. Higgins suggested talking with Dr. Ben Jorgensen who specializes in sensor arrays and Dr. Carol Baumbauer whose research involves printing flexible circuits. Her research could directly apply to printing a flexible pressure sensor array. However using such specialized components does eliminate the ability to procure replacement parts in the future. 
13.2.2 Wire Routing and Circuit Durability
Getting wires to different parts of the chair runs the risk of turning this thing into a rat's nest. Great care must be taken to ensure safe, strong, reliable, and maintainable wire routing. Wires could potentially be routed through the tubes of the frame which results in a very clean look and protects the wires. However in the event something goes wrong or needs to change, it's much more difficult to debug or maintain the electrical system. Another solution is using flexible conduit to capture wires into bundles and further insulate them from the frame. 
Due to the large impact loads these circuits are subjected to, a simple breadboard or even prototype board would get shaken to bits, leading Mr. Higgins to strongly recommend that we design and use a printed circuit board. This will drastically reduce the electronics complexity and size and increase its impact resistance at additional monetary cost. 
13.2.3 Emergency Stop
Another topic that received a lot of discussion was the state of the wheelchair after the emergency stop is triggered. As designed, the emergency stop cuts all power to everything. Naturally the teensy having no additional power supply or inductance will immediately shut down, leaving the rest of the system uncontrolled. This is potentially a safety hazard. The motors can continue unpredictable powered movement for short periods of time after the e-stop is triggered which could cause a safety hazard. For example if the chair moves at a high rate of speed and the e-stop is triggered and one wheel grinds to a halt but the other keeps going, the wheelchair could suddenly turn or flip over, which is clearly unacceptable. While that is a bit of an extreme case, it's not unrealistic for there to at least be some issues. However we cannot make any definitive judgement about this without testing it. One solution that was proposed was including a small capacitor bank to keep the teensy alive longer than the motors to control them until their momentum is dispersed. Overall we need to analyze this scenario and create a plan to work around it. 
13.2.4 Current Vs. Velocity Control
One brief note about control, according to Nardos, using current control as currently designed is non-trivial and requires a significant amount of code while we could use velocity control instead. Using velocity control would simplify our code and allow the motors to passively perform the current control. This is because the motor controller already intelligently handles all the math between our desired velocity and the current that needs to get sent to the motors.

15.2 Schedule Risks
The primary risks to the project schedule are associated with component procurement, fabrication dependencies, and subsystem integration. As no major components are currently in hand, delays in ordering or shipping could impact early assembly and integration efforts. To mitigate this risk, the team will prioritize purchasing of critical components at the beginning of the semester and identify alternative suppliers where possible.
Fabrication of custom components in the machine shop presents an additional risk due to potential scheduling constraints and the possibility of rework during iteration. The team will mitigate this by finalizing manufacturing drawings early and allowing buffer time for adjustments.
Finally, integration of mechanical, electrical, and control systems represents a key technical risk. As system functionality depends on successful coordination between subsystems, delays in one area may impact overall progress. To address this, the team will pursue parallel development across disciplines and aim to achieve early-stage integration by the 50% prototype milestone to enable iterative testing and reduce late-stage risk.
